# Conclusions - Regional Patterns in Canadian Dairy Genetic Evaluations

## What this study asked

Provincial genetic reports are usually read on their own, each describing one region's elite dairy population independently. This analysis combined the top 400 animals from all 10 Canadian provinces into a single dataset to ask a broader question: do regional differences in genetic merit go along with regional differences in the information behind those evaluations?

**"Information integration," used throughout this document, means the combination of pedigree, genomic testing, and official phenotype-based records (production and classification) behind an animal's genetic evaluation.** Genomic testing is one part of that combination - the results below treat it that way, as evidence for the broader integration pattern, not as the subject on its own.

Two more concepts matter throughout: an **Estimated Breeding Value (EBV)** is not a direct measurement - it's a statistical prediction of an animal's genetic merit, built from whatever information is available. **Reliability** reflects how much confidence to place in that prediction: how close it's likely to be to the animal's true genetic merit.

## Information behind genetic evaluations

Not every animal in this dataset carries the same weight of evidence. Crossing `LPI Code` (official phenotype-based evaluation vs. pedigree-only) with `GS` (genomic tested or not) gives four distinct information states, each with its own average reliability. Genomic information does not replace pedigree, it integrates with it, so each category below still includes pedigree:

| Category | Evidence available | `REL_PROT` | `REL_CONF` |
|---|---|---|---|
| PA | Pedigree-based evaluation | 38.0% | 35.3% |
| GPA | Pedigree + genomic information, no phenotype-based evaluation yet | 81.0% | 77.8% |
| EBV | Pedigree + phenotype-based information, no genomic test | 55.6% | 48.1% |
| GEBV | Pedigree + phenotype-based + genomic information | 84.1% | 81.1% |

GPA (pedigree plus genomic information) is associated with higher reliability than EBV (pedigree plus phenotype-based records, without genomics: 81.0% vs. 55.6% on protein) - a statement about precision, not about which source matters more biologically. GEBV (pedigree plus phenotype plus genomic information) is associated with the highest reliability of any combination. Genomic information does not replace pedigree, it integrates with it, so none of these four categories (PA, GPA, EBV, GEBV) represents "genomics alone" or "phenotype alone." (`EBV` status reflects production and classification records that may come from the animal itself or its relatives, such as daughters.)

**Genomic status is strongly associated with higher reliability in this dataset, more so than with the size of the predicted value itself.** The reliability gap between genomically tested and untested animals is very large (Cohen's d = 9.59 for protein reliability, 10.89 for conformation reliability: 81.4% vs. 40.0%, and 78.2% vs. 36.7%). This comparison groups all genomically tested animals together (mixing GPA and GEBV, and animals of different ages), so it should be read as an association between genomic status and reliability, not as an isolated, age-and-evaluation-status-controlled effect of genomic testing specifically. However, the reliability gap also remained very large when genomic and non-genomic animals were compared separately within `LPI Code` status (GPA vs. PA, and GEBV vs. EBV), a more controlled comparison, so the association is not simply an artifact of mixing groups. GEBV showed slightly higher reliability than GPA, indicating that reliability was greatest when genomic information was combined with official phenotype-based records in addition to pedigree, not when used with pedigree alone.

Separately, among animals restricted to age 2 or older, phenotype-based (EBV) and pedigree-based (PA) animals differed little in the predicted genetic values themselves: differences were small across the board (d = 0.03 to 0.12 for LPI, Pro$, Milk, Fat, and Protein, none reaching statistical significance at p<0.05), with only Conformation reaching statistical significance (d=0.22, p=0.0007). Pro$ was actually slightly lower, not higher, in EBV animals (d=-0.09), though not significantly so.

The highest-information group (genomic test + phenotype-based evaluation) ranges from just 2 animals in Manitoba to 67 in Ontario out of each province's 400 - a real difference in how many animals have evaluations supported by the most complete combination of available information.

**Method note:** provincial comparisons in this report use the unweighted mean of each province's 400 animals as the primary result. As a sensitivity check, provincial mean Conformation was recalculated using `REL_CONF` as an analytical weight (this weighting was applied to Conformation specifically, not to every trait or index in this report). The reliability-weighted sensitivity analysis did not materially change provincial mean Conformation: every weighted-versus-unweighted difference was smaller than 0.4 points (Saskatchewan +0.37, the largest; Manitoba -0.02, essentially unchanged). The GEBV-only reference mean remains unstable in provinces with very small subgroups, particularly Manitoba (n=2), but that is a separate issue from this weighted-mean sensitivity check, not evidence the weighting itself is unstable. Full method detail is in the statistical methodology document, Section 1.1.

## Regional patterns

A related regional pattern: the provincial share of genomically tested animals is associated with mean LPI. **Across all 10 provinces, genomic representation is associated with average LPI: r=0.73 (p=0.016), confirmed with a rank-based check (Spearman ρ=0.81, p=0.005).** Checking each province's residual from that trend line shows **Newfoundland & Labrador is a clear outlier**: it has the 5th-highest genomic testing rate (85.75%) but an average LPI about 207 points below what that testing rate would predict, about 2.4 times the size of the next-largest absolute residual (Ontario, at about 87 points). Excluding Newfoundland & Labrador specifically, the correlation among the remaining 9 provinces is much stronger (r=0.95, p=0.0001). Excluding Quebec instead (the province with the single highest genomic rate) leaves the relationship largely unchanged (r=0.71, p=0.033), confirming the result isn't an artifact of one high-testing province either.

This dataset cannot explain why Newfoundland & Labrador combines a relatively high genomic testing rate with a lower-than-expected average LPI. No province needs to be excluded from this comparison on data-quality grounds (all 10 show a plausible `LPI Code` split and a `GS` field that looks equally trustworthy across the board), so this is reported as a genuine, unresolved pattern rather than a data issue.

**Genomic representation was not significantly associated with LPI dispersion** (SD, the primary measure: r=0.13; CV sensitivity check: r=0.01) - provinces with more genomic testing are not more internally consistent, only higher-scoring on average. This dataset cannot determine why provinces differ in genomic representation to begin with - nothing here speaks to producer cost, awareness, or access. This specific analysis used only `% Genomic Representation`, not GEBV share, EBV share, average reliability, or the full PA/GPA/EBV/GEBV distribution together, so the precise conclusion is: provinces differ in genomic representation, and that representation is associated with mean LPI in these elite lists.

**By province** (ranked by average LPI):

- **Ontario** has the highest average LPI (3,884), alongside broadly strong performance on most other traits. On the exploratory type-versus-health/reproduction contrast it ranks 3rd (+1.33), behind New Brunswick and Nova Scotia.
- **Quebec** ranks 2nd in average LPI (3,858) but is the most internally consistent province in the dataset (CV=1.45%, the lowest of any province) - a distinct strength from Ontario's. Quebec sits close to neutral on the type-versus-health/reproduction contrast (-0.10), unlike the provinces at either extreme.
- **Manitoba** ranks 3rd (3,805) and shows the most negative score on the exploratory type-versus-health/reproduction contrast of any province (-2.93), the opposite direction from most others, but 64% of its top-400 sit below breed average on Dairy Strength. Its most complete information group (genomic + phenotype-based) is only 2 animals, so figures specific to that subgroup are suggestive rather than definitive.
- **British Columbia** ranks 4th (3,771), leaning toward health/reproduction on the contrast (-0.82).
- **Alberta** ranks 5th (3,717), leaning mildly toward health/reproduction on the contrast (-0.51), and statistically indistinguishable from Nova Scotia (confirmed by both Tukey HSD and Games-Howell).
- **Nova Scotia** ranks 6th (3,710), the 2nd-strongest lean toward type of any province (+1.35), statistically indistinguishable from Alberta despite the very different profile position.
- **Prince Edward Island** ranks 7th (3,608), leaning toward health/reproduction (-1.25) - the 2nd-most negative score after Manitoba - and statistically indistinguishable from Saskatchewan.
- **Saskatchewan** ranks 8th (3,606), leaning mildly toward type (+0.66), statistically indistinguishable from PEI despite the different profile position.
- **Newfoundland & Labrador** ranks 9th (3,558), essentially tied with New Brunswick (statistically indistinguishable by both Tukey HSD and Games-Howell), and by far the widest spread between its strongest and weakest animals of any province (LPI standard deviation, CV=2.76%, about 46% higher than the next-highest provincial CV, Nova Scotia's 1.89%). It is also the clear outlier in the genomic representation versus LPI relationship described above: high testing rate, lower than expected LPI.
- **New Brunswick** ranks 10th (3,557), but shows the strongest lean toward type of any province on the exploratory contrast (+1.44), and is statistically indistinguishable from Newfoundland & Labrador on LPI.

**A note on how close many of these rankings are:** several provinces sit within a few dozen LPI points of each other, and this specific ranking reflects one data snapshot (see Limitations). Three pairs are statistically indistinguishable at the 0.05 level (Alberta-Nova Scotia; Newfoundland & Labrador-New Brunswick; Prince Edward Island-Saskatchewan) despite different raw averages - treat small rank differences between adjacent provinces as noise, not a settled hierarchy.

**Multivariate grouping** (PCA on the six official LPI subindexes, `PI`/`LTI`/`HWI`/`RI`/`MI`/`EI`, cross-checked with two independent clustering methods): two axes explain about 85% of the cross-province pattern. PC1 (62%) reflects overall strength across all six subindexes together. PC2 (23%) is dominated by `LTI` (Longevity & Type Index) and `EI` (Environmental Impact Index) pulling in opposite directions, not "feed efficiency vs. conformation" as an earlier draft of this analysis labeled it, `EI` combines Methane Efficiency, Feed Efficiency, and Body Maintenance Requirements, not feed efficiency alone. **Two clusters receive the strongest silhouette support (0.305 vs. 0.257 for three).** A three-cluster exploratory solution is also interpretable and, with adequate KMeans initialization (`n_init=100`), **agrees perfectly with Ward hierarchical clustering (Adjusted Rand Index = 1.000)**. Because the analysis contains only 10 provincial observations, neither solution should be treated as a definitive classification. **Manitoba stands apart from every other province** in both solutions, the most defensible specific claim from this analysis.

## Biological interpretation: production and conformation

Every correlation checked between production/component traits (Milk, Fat, Protein, %F, %P) and conformation traits (Conf, MS, F&L, DS, RP) fell below 0.3 - the strongest was Milk vs. Dairy Strength at only r=0.26. This shows a weak linear association in this selected top-400 subset; it doesn't prove statistical or biological independence, and doesn't confirm a specific design intent behind these trait groups.

What conformation *is* associated with is more nuanced than first appeared. Conformation traits (`Conf`, `MS`, `F&L`) correlate strongly with `LTI` (r=0.79-0.88), but this is expected by construction: `LTI` is Lactanet's Longevity & Type Index, explicitly built to "enhance herd longevity favouring functional conformation," with Herd Life, Mammary System, Feet & Legs, Dairy Strength, and Rump as named components. Since conformation traits are literal ingredients of `LTI`, this correlation confirms the known formula rather than revealing something new, and this dataset has no separate Herd Life column to test the underlying belief independently.

An observed, non-definitional negative association within this selected dataset does emerge, though. `DS` (Dairy Strength) shows this with two subindexes that share no ingredients with it: `HWI` (Health & Welfare Index, r=-0.40) and `RI` (Reproduction Index, r=-0.46); overall `Conf` shows a similar, weaker pattern (r=-0.25 and r=-0.33). This is biologically consistent with published evidence on the same Canadian conformation trait system, though that research does not independently reproduce these exact correlations: Alcantara et al. (2022) found that Body Depth, a Dairy Strength component, was associated with lower Pro$ and cited studies showing Body Depth unfavorably correlated with productive life, number of lactations, fertility measures, and non-return rates in Holstein cattle. The same research also found a different Dairy Strength component, Dairy Capacity, associated positively with longevity, so the aggregated `DS` trait used in this dataset may combine sub-components pulling in different directions, something that can't be tested directly here since Body Depth and Dairy Capacity aren't available as separate columns. As noted in the Limitations section, this dataset selects only high-LPI animals, itself built partly from these subindexes, so part of this association may be induced by that selection rather than reflecting an unrestricted-population effect.

The intuitive idea that better udder conformation (`MS`) means better udder health specifically was tested directly: `MS` vs. `SCS` (the mastitis indicator) is r=-0.03, stably near zero, and `MS` vs. `HWI` is weak but consistently negative (r=-0.13), smaller than the `DS` relationships above. The clearest mechanical, non-health-related pattern is `DS` (Dairy Strength) vs. `BMR` (Body Maintenance Requirements, a feed-cost trait based on metabolic body weight): r=-0.61, stable. Given that higher `BMR` values indicate *lower* maintenance feed requirements, this direction is expected rather than surprising: animals with more body capacity (`DS`) have larger metabolic body weight and therefore genuinely need more feed for maintenance, exactly as Lactanet's own description of this trait predicts, an internal consistency check the data passes, not a novel biological trade-off, unlike the `DS`-`HWI`/`RI` pattern above.

This dataset doesn't demonstrate that conformation delivers a benefit beyond its built-in weight inside the LPI formula. Confirming an actual downstream benefit would need data this project doesn't have - culling age, calving-ease records, clinical mastitis case counts, or realized lifetime profitability.

Separately, checking which traits carry the most weight in a regression predicting LPI found a very high R² (0.961), but this needs an important caveat confirmed against Lactanet's own published LPI structure: LPI is officially built as a weighted sum of six subindexes (Production Index 40%, Longevity and Type Index 32%, Health and Welfare Index 8%, Reproduction Index 10%, Milkability Index 5%, Environmental Impact Index 5%). Most of the traits used in this regression are either direct components of those subindexes or closely related published traits: `PI` is directly composed of Fat Yield (60%) and Protein Yield (40%), with Milk Yield a correlated trait rather than a direct ingredient; `LTI`'s five direct components are Herd Life, Mammary System, Feet & Legs, Dairy Strength, and Rump, with overall Conformation a correlated trait rather than one of the five. The high R² mostly confirms that the model is structurally linked to how LPI is constructed, rather than an independent discovery of its determinants. An earlier version of this analysis also reported Milk Yield's contribution as "near zero" (a partial regression coefficient of 0.02), but Lactanet's own published correlation between Milk Yield and LPI is a real, moderate 0.43. The near-zero coefficient reflects multicollinearity between Milk, Fat, and Protein (all three closely related to the same subindex), not evidence that milk volume is unimportant to LPI. Fat and Protein are traits for which nearly all animals in this dataset exceed the stated reference value (only 0.05% of animals fall below breed average on either), versus 20.7% for Dairy Strength. Average LPI by birth-year cohort ranges narrowly (about 3,687 to 3,713) across the five cohorts with a reliable sample size (2022-2026, at least 136 animals each); the three earliest cohorts (2018-2021) show more variable averages, but their samples are too small (n=1 to 28) to draw a trend from, an earlier draft's claim of a rise from "~3,400 to ~3,800" is not supported by this data and has been corrected. Feed efficiency (FE) showed only a very weak link to body size or conformation (FE vs. Dairy Strength r=0.07; FE vs. Conf r=0.03, both barely reaching statistical significance given the large sample size).

## Practical implications

The central message here is not that one province breeds better animals than another. It's that these evaluations depend on integrating complementary sources of information - pedigree, genomic testing, and phenotype-based records each contribute different evidence, and where more of them are combined, evaluations tend to be more reliable. This dataset cannot estimate economic return - it contains predicted genetic values, not production, health, or financial records - so no ROI claim is made here.

The regional differences in how information sources are combined may represent an opportunity for province-specific knowledge-transfer or extension strategies emphasizing their complementary value, rather than one national message assuming uniform adoption. Whether those differences trace back to producer preference, historical breeding objectives, economics, or something else isn't something this dataset can settle - only that the pattern exists and lines up with measurable differences in evaluation quality.

The same national LPI formula is used to rank the provincial lists analyzed here, although individual herds may apply different or customized selection priorities. A shared formula, though, doesn't guarantee a shared profile: LPI is a weighted sum of 6 subindexes where `PI` and `LTI` alone total 72% of the weight, so two animals can reach an identical LPI through very different combinations of the underlying components. This is demonstrated directly in the notebook (Step 20.1): among the roughly 140 animals sharing essentially the same LPI in this dataset, individual subindex values differ by up to several hundred points, and one concrete pair shares the exact same LPI while showing a markedly different profile across the 6 subindexes. **LPI is informative about weighted overall merit, but it is not sufficient to describe the distribution of that merit across its six component domains.**

Step 20.2 builds an exploratory, equal-domain diagnostic of how evenly each animal's 6 subindexes sit relative to each other, cross-subindex dispersion. This is not part of the official LPI formula, it weights all 6 domains equally, which the real LPI explicitly does not do, and lower dispersion does not by itself mean a favorable profile (an animal weak across all 6 domains would also show low dispersion). Reading dispersion alongside overall level, Welch ANOVA finds a real but small provincial effect (about 4.6% of variance), and Games-Howell finds a small majority of province pairs (26 of 45) statistically different on this measure, a less clean-cut separation than for LPI itself. Saskatchewan and Nova Scotia show the lowest average dispersion; Ontario the highest. This analysis tests whether provincial elite profiles differ in cross-subindex evenness, it does not determine whether a less even profile is economically harmful, biologically undesirable, or intentionally specialized, that would require real herd outcomes (production, health events, longevity, profitability) this dataset doesn't have.

One further limitation applies broadly here: because every animal in this dataset was included specifically for having a high LPI, itself a weighted sum of these same subindexes, conditioning on this selected top-scoring group can induce apparent trade-offs among components that would be weaker or absent in an unrestricted population, an animal weak in one domain may need to be stronger elsewhere just to remain in the top 400. This applies to the `DS`-`HWI` and `DS`-`RI` associations reported earlier in this document too: they are well-supported *within this selected dataset*, but their generalizability to an unrestricted population is uncertain.

**By audience:**
- **Producers:** more reliable evaluations reduce uncertainty when selecting replacements or making culling decisions.
- **Geneticists:** the results highlight the complementary - not redundant - contribution of pedigree, genomic, and phenotypic information.
- **Breed organizations and AI/breeding companies:** regional differences in information integration point to where it may be worth better understanding how information is adopted and combined across breeding programs, and where sire/genetics marketing claims should be checked against the underlying reliability, not just the predicted value.
- **Researchers:** this proposes information integration, rather than provincial ranking, as a lens for interpreting elite genetic populations.

## Open questions and limitations

- **Not a census.** Each province's file is its top 400 animals ranked by LPI. Every "provincial average" describes each province's elite tier, not its general herd.
- **"Dataset average" ≠ an official national average** - every average here is computed from these 4,000 records only.
- **Every animal in this dataset is unique** - checked directly (pairwise ID comparison across all 10 provinces): 0 animals appear in more than one province's file.
- **This is a snapshot in time, not a static dataset.** Lactanet's official evaluations are released periodically, and which animals rank in each province's top 400 shifts as new births, milk recording, classification, and genomic results arrive. The selection criterion is each animal's LPI at the time of this data pull - rankings reported here, especially close ones between adjacent provinces, should be read as a point-in-time picture, not a permanent hierarchy.
- **`LPI Code` now shows a plausible PA/EBV split in all 10 provinces** - no province needs to be excluded from that comparison. Manitoba's genomic-and-phenotype sample is still only 2 animals, so figures specific to that subgroup are suggestive rather than definitive.
- **Genomic testing turnaround isn't captured** - a recently-sampled animal awaiting results (about a month) looks identical to one never tested, mainly affecting the youngest cohorts.
- **Age is approximate** (birth year only).
- **No direct body-size/height measurement exists** - `DS` and `Conf` were used as imperfect proxies.
- **"Consistency" was measured two different ways, with different answers:** between-animal consistency (Quebec highest, Newfoundland & Labrador lowest) is a different question from within-animal type-trait dispersion, using `MS`/`F&L`/`DS`/`RP` only (`Conf` excluded since it's built from these same four traits): Nova Scotia and Saskatchewan show the lowest dispersion (most even), Prince Edward Island close behind, while Manitoba and Newfoundland & Labrador show the highest (least even) - Quebec is only mid-to-lower on this second measure despite leading the first. Lower dispersion measures type-profile evenness, not conformation merit.

## Future research

This analysis used predicted genetic values only, drawn from top-ranked subsets rather than complete provincial populations. Useful next steps would include validating these regional patterns against complete national evaluation datasets, and combining these genetic evaluations with herd-level production, health, longevity, and economic records to test whether the patterns found here translate into measurable biological or economic outcomes over time.
